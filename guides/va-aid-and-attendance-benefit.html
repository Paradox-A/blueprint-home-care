VA Aid & Attendance: A Benefit That Can Help Pay for Home Care

Many wartime veterans and surviving spouses qualify for this VA pension benefit and never apply. Here's who's eligible, how paying for home care fits in, and how the application actually works.

Aid & Attendance isn't VA health care, and it isn't automatic. It's an increase added on top of an existing VA pension for veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily living — and it's one of the more overlooked ways to help a family afford care at home.

Please read before you apply

This page is background information, not legal, financial, or VA benefits advice, and not a promise of eligibility or any award amount. The income, asset, and medical-expense rules are genuinely complicated, and small documentation mistakes cause real delays. We strongly recommend applying with help from a free, VA-accredited Veteran Service Officer rather than alone — see "Also ask" below.

What Aid & Attendance Actually Is

Aid & Attendance and its close relative, Housebound, are monthly add-ons to a veteran's VA Pension or a surviving spouse's Survivors Pension — not stand-alone programs. You have to qualify for the underlying pension first; the add-on then increases the monthly amount for someone who needs help with daily activities. VA pays one or the other, never both at once.

Who Can Qualify

Wartime service (veterans)

Generally, you need active-duty service with at least one day during a VA-recognized wartime period, discharged under conditions other than dishonorable. Veterans who entered active duty after September 7, 1980 generally face a longer minimum service-length requirement. VA's eligibility page has the exact periods and exceptions for your situation — they're specific enough that it's worth checking rather than assuming.

Surviving spouses

A surviving spouse of a veteran who met the wartime service requirement may qualify for Survivors Pension, which carries its own Aid & Attendance or Housebound add-on.

The financial test

VA counts your (and your spouse's, if married) net worth plus annual income against a limit that's adjusted every December with the Social Security cost-of-living increase. For the rate year running December 1, 2025 through November 30, 2026, that net worth limit is $163,699 — and it will change again in December 2026, so check the current figure before you plan around it. Your primary home, one vehicle, and ordinary household items generally don't count toward that limit, though the exact exclusions have nuances worth confirming.

The medical/functional test for Aid & Attendance

You (or the veteran/spouse the claim is for) generally need at least one of the following: help from another person with daily activities like bathing, eating, or dressing; being confined to bed for much of the day because of illness; living in a nursing home due to loss of mental or physical capacity; or significantly limited eyesight even with correction. Housebound, the alternative add-on, applies when a permanent disability substantially confines someone to their home instead.

Where Home Care Costs Fit In

VA doesn't just look at raw income — it subtracts unreimbursed medical expenses (UMEs) you pay out of pocket, above a small threshold, before comparing what's left to the income limit. For some families, that deduction is what turns an income that looks "too high" on paper into one that qualifies.

Payments to an in-home caregiver for help with daily activities can count as a UME, but the rules for exactly which caregiver costs qualify depend on details like whether you already hold an Aid & Attendance or Housebound rating and whether the caregiver is a licensed provider, and VA expects documentation — a receipt or provider statement showing the amount, date, and purpose of each payment. This is exactly the kind of detail that's easy to get wrong without help, and we are not positioned to give you a ruling on your own paperwork. An accredited Veteran Service Officer, VA-accredited claims agent, or attorney can tell you what will and won't count in your specific case.

How to Apply, Step by Step

1.Gather discharge papers (DD-214) showing dates of service.
2.Ask the veteran's or spouse's doctor to complete VA Form 21-2680, the medical examination report VA uses to evaluate Housebound or Aid & Attendance status. This is a clinical determination — it has to come from a physician, not from us or from a checklist.
3.Complete VA Form 21P-527EZ (Application for Pension, for veterans) or VA Form 21P-534EZ (for surviving spouses).
4.Mail both forms to the VA Pension Intake Center, PO Box 5365, Janesville, WI 53547-5365 — or bring them in person to a VA regional office.
5.Consider appointing a free, VA-accredited Veteran Service Officer, claims agent, or attorney (VA Form 21-22) to help prepare the claim and track it through to a decision.

Processing commonly takes several months. Ask whoever helps you file how effective dates work for your specific claim — VA's rules on backdating an approved award to an earlier filing date are detailed enough that they're worth confirming for your situation rather than assuming.
