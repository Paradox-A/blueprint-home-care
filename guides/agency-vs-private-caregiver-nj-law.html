Agency or Private Hire? What New Jersey Law Says About Who's the Employer

The legal and liability differences most families never hear about until something goes wrong — explained in plain language.

When a family decides it's time to bring in help, the choice often gets framed as "agency versus hiring someone ourselves." What rarely gets explained is that this is also a legal choice: it decides who, under New Jersey and federal law, becomes the caregiver's employer — and who inherits the paperwork, tax obligations, and liability that come with that role.

Please read before you decide

This guide explains how New Jersey and federal law generally treat these two arrangements. It is educational information, not legal or tax advice, and it isn't a substitute for talking with an employment attorney or accountant about your specific situation. Laws and thresholds referenced here change — verify current details at the sources listed at the bottom of this page.

Two Paths, Two Different Employers

Broadly, families choose between two models:

  • A registered home care agency employs or refers the caregiver and remains the business responsible for the arrangement — payroll, insurance, screening, and coverage when someone calls out.
  • A privately hired, independent caregiver is paid directly by the family, often found through word of mouth, a classified ad, or a caregiver-matching website. In this arrangement, the family itself typically takes on the legal role of employer.

Neither path is against the law. But each comes with real obligations, and it's worth understanding both before you commit to one.

What "Registered Health Care Service Firm" Actually Means

Under New Jersey law (N.J.S.A. 34:8-43 et seq.), a business that employs, places, or in any way refers workers to provide companion, home care, or personal care services inside a private home — for a client who is 60 or older or has a disability — must register with the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs as a Health Care Service Firm (HCSF). Registered firms are also required to obtain accreditation from a body recognized by the state within roughly a year of registering. Blueprint's own registration number, HP0441400, is this designation at work.

This registration requirement is specific to firms — it doesn't apply to a family hiring one person directly. That's exactly why it's worth asking any agency you're considering for their current HCSF registration number and confirming it directly with the Division of Consumer Affairs, rather than taking a website claim at face value.

If You Hire Directly, You May Become a "Household Employer"

The IRS treats anyone who hires a worker to perform services in their home, and who controls what work is done and how, as a household employer — this includes a privately hired caregiver, not just a nanny or housekeeper. Per IRS Publication 926 (the Household Employer's Tax Guide), that generally means the family, not the caregiver, is responsible for obtaining an employer identification number, and — once the caregiver's cash wages for the year cross the threshold the IRS publishes annually in that guide — for withholding and matching Social Security and Medicare taxes, with possible federal unemployment tax obligations on top. Because that dollar threshold is adjusted periodically, check the current-year edition of Publication 926 at irs.gov rather than relying on a number you saw somewhere else.

Some families try to sidestep this by calling a privately hired caregiver a "1099 independent contractor." Whether that label actually holds up, however, isn't up to the family — it's decided by the tests described next.

New Jersey's "ABC Test" Doesn't Care What the Paperwork Says

For decisions under New Jersey's Unemployment Compensation Law (N.J.S.A. 43:21-19(i)(6)), a worker is presumed to be an employee unless the hiring party proves all three of the following:

  • (A) the worker is free from control or direction over how the work is performed;
  • (B) the work is either outside the usual course of the hiring party's business, or performed outside all of the hiring party's places of business; and
  • (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, profession, or business of that same nature.

In Hargrove v. Sleepy's, LLC, 220 N.J. 289 (2015), the New Jersey Supreme Court held that this same ABC test — not just a looser "right to control" standard — also governs independent-contractor status under New Jersey's Wage and Hour Law and Wage Payment Law, not only unemployment claims.

In practice, a caregiver who works regularly in one family's home, on a schedule the family sets, doing exactly the caregiving the family needs, will often struggle to satisfy prong (B) or (C) — regardless of what a signed agreement between the family and the caregiver calls the relationship. A contract that labels someone a "contractor" does not control the outcome if the actual working relationship looks like employment.

The 2024 Domestic Workers' Bill of Rights Changed the Rules Again

New Jersey's Domestic Workers' Bill of Rights (N.J.S.A. 34:11-69 et seq.), effective July 1, 2024, added new obligations for households that hire someone like a caregiver directly, with narrow exceptions for very occasional or casual work. Among them:

  • A written contract is generally required between the household and the worker, provided in English and in the worker's preferred language, covering duties, wages and overtime, schedule, breaks, leave, and other specified terms.
  • The contract may not include certain clauses, such as mandatory pre-dispute arbitration or non-disparagement provisions.
  • Household employers are generally required to carry workers' compensation insurance covering the worker.

When a family works with a registered agency instead, it's the agency — as the employing or referring firm — that typically carries this coverage and handles this paperwork, not the family.

Where the Responsibility Actually Lands

Working With a Registered Agency

  • The agency is generally the employer (or registered referring firm)
  • Agency typically handles payroll taxes and insurance
  • Agency is responsible for its own screening and accreditation standards
  • Agency usually arranges coverage if a caregiver is unavailable

Hiring a Caregiver Directly

  • The family is generally the employer
  • Family is typically responsible for payroll taxes, an EIN, and insurance
  • Family is responsible for its own screening decisions
  • Family generally arranges its own backup when a caregiver is unavailable

Whichever Path You Choose

Keep the scope of care non-medical. A caregiver — whether employed by an agency or hired directly — should provide companionship, personal care, and medication reminders, not medication management or clinical judgment calls. Any question about a medication, a new symptom, or a change in condition belongs with the person's own physician or a licensed home health agency, not with whoever happens to be in the home.

Verify Before You Sign Anything

Confirm an agency's current HCSF registration directly with the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs rather than relying on a website claim.

Check the current-year IRS Publication 926 at irs.gov for this year's household-employer tax thresholds before assuming any figure is still accurate.

If you're unsure how New Jersey's ABC test would apply to your situation, or what the Domestic Workers' Bill of Rights requires of you specifically, a New Jersey employment attorney or accountant can give you an answer tailored to your circumstances — this guide can't.
