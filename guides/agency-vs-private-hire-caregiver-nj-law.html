Hiring a Caregiver Privately vs. Through an Agency: What New Jersey Law Says About Who's Liable

Paying a caregiver "under the table" doesn't make them a contractor under New Jersey law. It's worth understanding what that actually means before you decide how to hire.

Many families weighing home care eventually ask some version of: "Could we just hire someone ourselves and skip the agency?" It's a fair question, and private arrangements work fine for plenty of families. But the decision isn't only about cost or control — it's also a legal one, and New Jersey has been tightening the rules around it. This guide walks through what the law actually requires on each path, in plain language, using the actual statutes rather than general assumptions.

This is not legal or tax advice, and it isn't a pitch to scare you into one option. It's the information an elder-law attorney or accountant would eventually walk you through anyway — just earlier, and for free.

1. "Independent Contractor" Isn't Your Choice to Make

A common assumption is that if a privately hired caregiver agrees to be paid as a 1099 independent contractor, that settles the question. Under New Jersey law, it doesn't. New Jersey's Unemployment Compensation Law presumes every paid worker is an employee unless the person paying them can prove all three parts of what's called the "ABC test" (N.J.S.A. 43:21-19(i)(6)(A)–(C)):

  • (A) the worker is free from the hiring party's control or direction over how the work is performed;
  • (B) the work is either outside the usual course of the hiring party's business, or performed outside all the hiring party's places of business; and
  • (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, profession, or business.

A caregiver who works set hours in your parent's home, follows a schedule and routine the family sets, and doesn't run their own independent care business almost never clears all three prongs. In Hargrove v. Sleepy's, LLC (2015), the New Jersey Supreme Court confirmed that this same ABC test — not a looser federal standard — governs whether someone is an employee for wage-and-hour and benefits purposes generally, not just unemployment claims.

In plain terms: writing "independent contractor" on a piece of paper, or paying cash, doesn't change what the caregiver legally is in the eyes of the state. If the arrangement looks like employment, New Jersey treats it as employment — and that triggers the obligations below, whether or not anyone intended it to.

2. What Hiring Privately Actually Obligates You To Do

New Jersey's Domestic Workers' Bill of Rights Act (P.L. 2023, c. 262, signed January 12, 2024, effective July 1, 2024) removed a long-standing exclusion that had left household workers — explicitly including companions and caretakers for elderly, sick, or disabled family members — outside the state's standard wage, hour, and anti-discrimination protections. Practically, if you hire a caregiver directly as a household employer, current New Jersey Department of Labor and Workforce Development guidance for domestic employers says you need to:

Household employer obligations under current NJ guidance

Obtain a federal Employer Identification Number (EIN); register for New Jersey payroll taxes; and carry workers' compensation insurance once you've paid a domestic worker above the state's reporting threshold (some homeowners' insurance policies include limited workers' comp coverage that can satisfy this — ask your insurer to confirm). You're also expected to put the arrangement in a written agreement once the caregiver works more than a small number of hours a month, provide legally required rest and meal breaks, and give proper notice before ending the arrangement, particularly for live-in care.

Separately, the IRS treats a household caregiver the same way it treats a nanny: once you pay a household employee above an annual cash-wage threshold the IRS sets and periodically updates, you owe Social Security and Medicare taxes (split between you and the caregiver), and you may owe federal unemployment tax above a separate quarterly threshold. This is reported on Schedule H of your federal tax return. The current thresholds and the full mechanics are in IRS Publication 926, Household Employer's Tax Guide — worth reading in full, or reviewing with an accountant, before you set up a private arrangement.

3. What a Registered Home Care Agency Carries Instead

New Jersey's Health Care Service Firm Licensing Act (N.J.S.A. 34:8-45.1 et seq., with registration rules at N.J.A.C. 13:45B-13.1 et seq.) requires any firm that places or refers caregivers into the home of a person who is 60 or older or living with a disability to register as a Health Care Service Firm (HCSF) with the state's Division of Consumer Affairs. Registered firms are also required to obtain formal health care accreditation from a state-approved accrediting body within a set period after registering, and to carry a surety bond.

When you hire through a registered agency rather than directly, the agency — not your family — is generally the legal employer. That shifts payroll tax withholding, workers' compensation coverage, and (in most cases) general and professional liability insurance for the caregiver's work onto the agency rather than onto you personally. It's also why the single most useful question in our hiring questions checklist is simply: "Are you registered as a Health Care Service Firm with the State of New Jersey?"

4. Side by Side

Hiring Privately

  • You are generally the legal employer under the ABC test, regardless of any contract that says otherwise.
  • You're responsible for your own EIN, NJ payroll tax registration, and workers' comp coverage.
  • You handle IRS "nanny tax" obligations yourself (Schedule H).
  • No built-in backup caregiver if yours is sick, quits, or is unavailable.
  • You're responsible for running your own background check.
  • More control over who is hired and the exact arrangement.

Hiring Through a Registered Agency

  • The agency is generally the legal employer, with its own payroll and tax obligations.
  • The agency must register as a Health Care Service Firm and carry a surety bond.
  • The agency typically carries liability insurance and runs its own screening before placement.
  • An agency usually has backup coverage if your regular caregiver is unavailable — ask how, specifically.
  • Less control over exactly who is assigned, though most agencies allow input and introductions first.
“Neither path is automatically the 'right' one — but only one of them leaves the tax and liability paperwork sitting on your kitchen table.”

5. Questions Worth Asking Either Way

  • If hiring privately: have we registered for an EIN and NJ payroll taxes, and do we have workers' comp coverage in place (through our homeowner's policy or separately)?
  • If hiring privately: have we reviewed current IRS Publication 926 thresholds with an accountant, and do we have a written agreement in place?
  • If hiring through an agency: is the agency currently registered as a Health Care Service Firm, and can they show proof?
  • Either way: what happens, specifically, if the caregiver is sick, injured, or simply doesn't work out?

A Note on Accuracy

Nothing here is medical, legal, or tax advice, and none of it is a knock on families who choose to hire privately — it's a legitimate choice many families make well. The point of this guide is simply that it's a choice with real legal substance behind it, not just a cheaper version of the same arrangement. Confirm current thresholds, forms, and requirements directly with the NJ Department of Labor and Workforce Development, the IRS, or an attorney before acting.
